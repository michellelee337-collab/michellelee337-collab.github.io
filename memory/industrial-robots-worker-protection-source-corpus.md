## Cross-source findings

### 1. Existing safety law for fixed robots

Most respondents agree that Korean occupational-safety law has a basic framework for conventional, fixed industrial robots: risk assessment, guarding, interlocks, inspection, training, shutdown, and maintenance procedures. The disagreement is whether that framework is already sufficient if enforced or only a partial baseline.

### 2. New robot types create the central regulatory gap

The repeated gap concerns collaborative robots, autonomous mobile robots, AI-controlled robots, and humanoids working in the same space as people. Sources repeatedly identify limits of fence-based rules, along with sensor, software, autonomous-decision, remote-stop, maintenance, and responsibility-allocation questions. Several add psychosocial and labor-intensity risks: monitoring stress, pace pressure, isolation, constant readiness, and algorithmic control.

### 3. Enforcement versus law on the books

A substantial counterposition says the statutes and guidance are already broad enough, while accidents arise because safety devices are bypassed, risk assessments and committees are formalities, workers and employers lack awareness, and supervision is weak. This view appears in the calls with 김희연, anonymous certified labor attorney A, 안찬호, and 정재혁, and in several emails and survey responses.

### 4. Participation exists formally but is usually weak

The dominant view is that labor-management councils, occupational safety and health committees, risk assessments, and collective bargaining provide possible channels, but consultation is often nonbinding, late, formal, or absent. Robot introduction is usually treated as management prerogative. The gap is largest in small, nonunion, subcontracted, or contingent workplaces. Several respondents distinguish consultation from consent or joint decision-making.

### 5. Worker protection is mainly reactive

Existing dismissal, transfer, work-rule, and unfair-personnel-action doctrines can provide after-the-fact remedies. Respondents repeatedly say they do not create a clear pre-deployment right to information, consultation, training, transition time, income preservation, or priority placement. Employer discretion over reassignment is usually described as broad unless material disadvantage can be proved.

### 6. Reassignment is often the practical harm

The recurring pattern is not immediate mass dismissal. It is voluntary retirement, nonreplacement of attrition, reduced new hiring, transfer to unrelated sales or office work, loss of overtime allowances, assignment to low-value support tasks, adverse performance evaluation in an unfamiliar role, and eventual resignation. Existing workers may remain employed while losing skill value, career development, meaningful work, or earnings.

### 7. Common proposals

Repeated proposals include pre-deployment safety and labor-impact assessment, earlier information disclosure, meaningful worker participation, binding or better-defined consultation, updated standards for collaborative and mobile AI robots, mandatory retraining and transition support, income support during training, employer or public funding for just transition, clearer manufacturer and operator responsibility, stronger enforcement, and special attention to small firms and subcontracting chains.

### 8. Important counterarguments

Several respondents warn against treating worker protection as the only value. They stress management rights, competitiveness, the safety benefits of replacing hazardous human work, the difficulty of legislating faster than technology, and the risk of rigid rules encouraging offshoring or reduced hiring. Some prefer incentives, public training, and practical guidance over new prohibitions.

### 9. Case patterns in the corpus

Attributed examples include workers moved from production to unfamiliar sales or office roles, loss of overtime or special-work allowances, reassignment followed by low-performance labeling, workers leaving after an automated line created surplus labor, automated systems reducing new hiring, maintenance and repair accidents, increased monitoring and stress, and unions learning of technology decisions only after plans were substantially fixed. These examples require consent and primary verification before publication.
