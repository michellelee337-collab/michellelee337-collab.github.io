## 2. Publication entry (full, as implemented on pagoda.html)

**"A Pagoda"** — *Teachers & Writers Magazine* (New York), April 15, 2026 · **2026 Bechtel Prize honorable mention**

A reflective essay on teaching creative writing in Cambodia. In a crowded classroom that loses power mid-lesson — with mothers and grandmothers sitting in — Michelle builds a writing course from the inside out: a read-aloud of *The Grand Hotel of Feelings*, a feelings-and-needs inventory to give students emotional vocabulary, identity trees branching from "I am," letters to their mothers in vivid sensory language, and finally the transcription of Cambodian folktales in both Khmer and English. The essay's argument is its method: students don't need to borrow stories from elsewhere to become writers — they need a classroom that treats their emotions, families, languages, and cultural memories as literary material, and a teacher who listens first.

**Key themes:** multilingual literacy · Khmer–English learning · emotional vocabulary · identity writing · oral tradition · narrative pedagogy · student voice · community-based education

**Why it matters in her body of work:** it is the philosophy underneath everything else — the reason Lumen publishes folktales children already know, the reason her translation work treats language as a bridge, and the reason her legal direction centers on making systems legible. One essay, one classroom, and the whole through-line of the site becomes visible.

## 3. Teaching philosophy (final copy — implemented)

> Michelle's teaching begins with listening. The creative-writing classroom she describes in "A Pagoda" — her Bechtel Prize–recognized essay for *Teachers & Writers Magazine* — starts not with grammar but with a feelings-and-needs vocabulary, identity trees, and letters to mothers, because students must first believe they have a voice worth writing with. English, in her classroom, does not replace Khmer; it works as a bridge, another tool for naming emotions, preserving memory, and telling stories rooted in family and community. Writing is treated as construction — word by word, like stones fitted into a pagoda — and the classroom as a safe place to build: for students whose lives are otherwise unstable, the page is one space they wholly decide for themselves.

## 4. Lumen connection (final copy — implemented on lumen.html)

> The classroom described in "A Pagoda" is the philosophy behind Lumen in miniature. Michelle's students moved from naming feelings to writing letters to their mothers to transcribing Cambodian folktales in Khmer and English — and Lumen industrialized exactly that arc: children do not need to borrow stories from elsewhere to become readers and writers, because their families, villages, languages, and memories are already full of literary material.
