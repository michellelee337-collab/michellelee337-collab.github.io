## ABOUT MICHELLE (full bio — also serves as About page copy)

Michelle Lee (Minjung Lee) is an educator, writer, and translator whose work begins with a simple conviction: access is everything — access to learning, to language, and to the law.

She grew up moving between languages and countries, and that early experience of translation — of carrying meaning across a border — became the through-line of her work. She holds a B.A. in International Studies with a concentration in International Law and Diplomacy from Ewha Womans University, including an exchange term at the University of Exeter in autumn 2023, and is currently pursuing an LL.B. at Korea Cyber University as she prepares for U.S. law school.

In the classroom, Michelle has taught English, social studies, reading, writing, debate, and AP subjects to students across age groups, in person and online, in Korean and English. [Placeholder: one sentence naming where she has taught — school/academy/program names and years.] Teaching showed her how unevenly opportunity is distributed — and pushed her work beyond the classroom. In 2021 she co-founded Angels for the Journey, a youth-led initiative providing long-term tuition and educational assistance to vulnerable children and families in the Philippines, Cambodia, and Laos. In 2025 she co-founded Lumen Education in Cambodia, where she [placeholder: her specific Lumen role — e.g., edits/writes/translates the books, designs reading curriculum, coordinates library partnerships] for multilingual children's books and community libraries.

Alongside education, language access anchors her service. Since 2018 she has volunteered as a community translator with AsianHub, working with immigrants and multicultural families in Korea, and she has supported international students as an exchange coordinator with ISC Global. As a youth journalist and later intern at LeadersTimes, and as a translator of political and constitutional reporting for MEDIAWATCH, she has written and translated stories about historical memory — from war remembrance to press freedom — guided by a question she keeps returning to: how do laws and policies look from the perspective of the people they are written about?

That question is now taking her toward public-interest law, with a focus on labor rights, migrant communities, children, and language access. Wherever the work leads, its shape stays the same: standing between a person and a barrier, and translating.

## TOWARD PUBLIC-INTEREST LAW (narrow prose section)

Years of teaching, translating, and community work keep leading Michelle to the same observation: the people most affected by laws are often the last to be able to read them. A migrant worker's contract, a multicultural family's school paperwork, a child's route to a classroom — each is governed by rules written in a language, and a register, its subjects were never given.

Her concentration in International Law and Diplomacy at Ewha, her current LL.B. study, and her translation of constitutional reporting have turned that observation into a direction: public-interest law, with particular attention to labor rights, migrant communities, children, and language access.

She approaches law the way she approaches translation — with the belief that meaning belongs to the people it's about. *How do laws and policies look from the perspective of the people they are written about?* That is the question her legal education is built around, and the one she hopes to spend a career answering.
