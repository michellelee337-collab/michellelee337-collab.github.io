## The question

South Korea has one of the highest industrial-robot densities in the world. Automation there is not a forecast; it is the current condition of manufacturing work. This ongoing research project asks a deliberately practical question: **when industrial robots arrive on a factory floor, what protection does Korean labor law actually give the workers affected — and where do the professionals who handle these cases see it falling short?**

The study does not ask whether robots should be stopped. It asks what happens to the people working next to them.

## Three kinds of protection

The research breaks "worker protection" into three concrete dimensions:

1. **Safety** — collision, entrapment, and maintenance accidents; but also the less visible risks: increased work pace, musculoskeletal strain, and data-driven monitoring of performance.
2. **Participation** — whether workers, unions, and labor-management councils receive information and a genuine chance to consult *before* robots are introduced, not after.
3. **Transition** — what actually happens to redeployment, retraining, wages, and job security once automation changes what a job is.

## Why it matters

Industrial robots rarely eliminate a workforce overnight. Instead they quietly rewrite the terms of work — the pace, the required skills, the safety envelope, the way performance is measured. The costs of that rewriting fall unevenly: older workers, mid-skill production workers, subcontracted and non-regular workers, and migrant workers are least positioned to absorb it. Michelle's concern for this last group is not abstract — her engagement with migrant communities, including concern for Sri Lankan migrant workers whose employment prospects can be narrowed by automation and shifting labor demand, is part of what shaped the research question. *(Careful wording — no representation claims.)*

## The analytical frame

The project borrows a lens from U.S. debates: Lina Khan's critique of Amazon argued that if you measure only consumer benefit, the costs borne by workers and suppliers become invisible. Amazon's warehouses — where automation raised productivity alongside injury rates, pace pressure, and algorithmic monitoring, drawing OSHA scrutiny — serve as a comparison point, not a subject. The Korean question becomes: **when automation makes firms and consumers better off, what costs does it shift onto workers — and can current law see them?**

## The legal terrain analyzed

- **Occupational Safety and Health Act (산업안전보건법)** — how well robot-specific safety rules cover physical risks, and whether pace, strain, and psychosocial risks are addressed at all.
- **Workers' Participation and Cooperation Act (근로자참여법)** — whether labor-management councils treat technology adoption as a real agenda item or a formality.
- **Trade Union Act (노동조합법)** — the crucial practical line between bargaining over *whether* robots are introduced (likely managerial prerogative) and bargaining over the *effects* of introduction (working conditions — arguably negotiable).
- **Labor Standards Act (근로기준법)** — redeployment, restructuring, and dismissal law when automation is the stated reason.
- **Vocational training and transition-support institutions** — whether retraining exists in practice or mostly on paper.

## Method

Literature review → Korean statutory analysis → U.S. comparison → short semi-structured interviews (10–15 minutes, five core questions) with **certified labor attorneys (공인노무사)** — licensed Korean professionals who specialize in labor law, workplace compliance, and labor-management relations, working across employer-side advisory, worker- and union-side cases, manufacturing workplaces, industrial-accident cases, and restructuring practice. Responses are anonymized, thematically coded, and compared across employer-side and worker-side practice backgrounds; no client, company, or case identities are recorded.

The five interview questions probe: the most important legal issue raised by robot adoption; whether current labor and safety law responds adequately; which information-sharing and consultation procedures matter in practice; what legal issues arise when automated equipment and data-based management affect pace, safety, or evaluation; and which protection area Korean labor law should examine first as robots spread.

## Current status & direction

The project is in progress. Interim expectations from the proposal — to be tested, not asserted: that existing law handles physical safety better than it handles consultation, transition, or data-driven management; that disputes cluster around redeployment and evaluation rather than dismissal; and that consultation bodies work only as well as workplace structures allow. Findings will be reported as the perceptions of practitioners, presented with frequency analysis and anonymized quotations — a deliberately modest evidentiary claim.
